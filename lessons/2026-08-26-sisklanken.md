# Cheat sheet — s · sz · ś

Student sheet: `Downloads/flashcards/lessons/2026-08-26-sisklanken.html`, in
Dutch. English below, for the teacher.

Written for the lesson of 2026-08-26, on the evidence of 120 ear trials:
`sz`↔`ś` accounts for 11 of his 14 sibilant errors, while `s` is 27 for 27.

## Why it is built this way

**Dutch first, English second.** He already produces both sounds — Dutch
`sj` in *meisje* is alveolo-palatal, close to `ś`, and Dutch `sj` in *sjaal*
is post-alveolar, close to `sz`. He simply never has to tell them apart,
because in Dutch the choice carries no meaning. That is a far better starting
point than "this is a new Polish sound", and it is the sheet's opening line.

**The lips are the headline, not the tongue.** `sz` is produced with lip
protrusion, `ś` without. It is the one cue he can *see* — in a mirror, or on
a speaker's face — which makes it checkable without any ear training at all.

**No acoustics.** An earlier draft told him to listen to the second formant
of the following vowel. That was true and useless: 18 of the 39 words have
the sibilant word-finally, including `koś`, `paś` and `wieś`, and two of his
three worst pairs are exactly those. The cue is absent where he needs it
most. The sheet says "listen to the colour of the hiss — thin and high, or
dark and hollow" instead, and tells him explicitly that the word-final ones
are hardest *because* there is no vowel to help.

**`s` is dismissed in one line.** He has it. Spending sheet space on it would
imply a problem he does not have.

## Full English translation

> ### s · sz · ś — three Polish hissing sounds
> *You already make all three — in Polish the difference means something*
>
> In Dutch you make these sounds too, but it doesn't matter which one you
> choose. In Polish they are different words.
>
> **The three sounds**
>
> **s** — as in *sok*, *huis* — ordinary Dutch.
> High and sharp. This one you already do fine.
> *sok · kos · pas · nos*
>
> **ś** — like the *sj* in *meisje*, *huisje*, *pilsje*.
> Tongue tip **down**, behind your lower teeth. Lips normal, almost a smile.
> Soft and thin.
> *koś · paś · wieś · noś*
>
> **sz** — like the *sj* in *sjaal*, or English *sh* in *shoe*.
> Tongue tip **up** and back. Lips pushed forward a little. Dark and hollow.
> *kosz · pasz · wiesz · szok*
>
> **The difference you can see**
>
> Stand in front of the mirror and say *meisje* — then *sjaal*.
>
> | ś (meisje) | lips normal, wide |
> | sz (sjaal) | lips forward, as if about to whistle |
>
> > This is the easiest check there is, because you can **see** it. Not sure
> > about a word? Look at the speaker's lips, or your own.
>
> **The difference you can hear**
>
> Do **not** listen to "how much hissing" — they hiss equally hard. Listen to
> the **colour** of the hiss:
>
> | ś | **high and thin** — almost like s, but softer |
> | sz | **low and hollow** — darker, fuller, further back |
>
> s against the other two comes by itself: s sits clearly higher than both.
> The hard part is only between **ś** and **sz**.
>
> **Your difficult pairs**
>
> | koś / kosz | mow! / basket |
> | paś / pasz | graze! / of fodder |
> | Kasia / kasza | Kasia / groats |
> | wieś / wiesz | village / you know |
>
> Note: in *koś*, *paś* and *wieś* the sound is at the **end** of the word.
> There's no vowel afterwards to help you — you have to hear it in the hiss
> itself. That's exactly why these are the hardest.
>
> **Practice at home**
>
> 1. Say *huisje* and hold the sj: *huissssj…* — that is **ś**.
> 2. Say *sjaal* and hold it: *ssshhh…* — that is **sz**.
> 3. Alternate in front of the mirror: ś — sz — ś — sz. Watch your lips.
> 4. Now with words: **wieś — wiesz — wieś — wiesz**. And: **koś — kosz**.
> 5. Have someone say one without you seeing. Guess which.
>
> **You already make both sounds.** You only have to learn to keep them
> apart.

## What the research says, for the teacher

- **HVPT works**, and is what the ear trainer is: g = 0.92 pretest–posttest,
  g = 0.67 against controls, across 79 studies. Real words beat nonsense
  words. Perception-only training transfers to production (d = 0.52).
- **More talkers is the biggest lever available**: >10 talkers gives g = 0.79.
  The trainer has two. Getting a few more people to read the sheet would do
  more than any amount of extra trials.
- **Articulatory instruction also improves perception**, not just production —
  the benefit runs both ways, which is why the sheet leads with the tongue and
  the lips rather than with listening.
- **Visual support helps** (g = 0.75), hence the mirror.
- **The acoustic reason it is hard**: `s` has a spectral centre of gravity
  around 8 kHz and is cleanly separated; `sz` and `ś` both sit near 3 kHz and
  are hard to tell apart from frication shape alone. He is failing the one
  contrast that is genuinely hardest, not being careless.
